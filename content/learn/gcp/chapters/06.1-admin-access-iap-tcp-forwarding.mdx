<Box kind="problem" title="The problem">

Finding 2: `kade-allow-ssh` allows port 22 from `0.0.0.0/0` to every VM. And because no VM has an external IP, the team's habit when they need a shell is to add a temporary external IP, SSH in, and (sometimes) remove it afterwards. For those minutes, the VM is open to every SSH scanner on the internet: automated programs that try every public address, all day, looking for an SSH port that will let them in.

The goal: SSH to any VM with no external IP, no open port, and no extra server to maintain. And without locking the team out while making the change.

</Box>

<Figure wide hues={["amber", "teal", "green"]} labels={{ amber: "The bastion: the one VM exposed to SSH", teal: "kade-vpc and its private VMs", green: "Your SSH hops" }} drawing={<FigBastion />}>
The bastion pattern. You SSH to the bastion, then hop from it to the VMs behind it.

A bastion has three real costs, numbered in the drawing:

1. **It is a VM you run.** You keep it updated, lock it down and pay for it, and it is only as safe as that upkeep.
2. **It is still an SSH port open to the internet**, just one instead of many. Every scanner finds it and keeps trying.
3. **If an attacker takes it over, they are already inside the network.** That can happen through a stolen SSH key, a guessed password or an unpatched bug. From the bastion they can reach every VM behind it, exactly as you do.

A bastion reduces exposure to one box. It does not remove it.

## What IAP TCP forwarding is

<Term>Identity-Aware Proxy (IAP)</Term> is a service Google runs for you. It is not a VM in your project: there is nothing to install, size or patch. It runs on Google's own servers, inside Google's network, and Google keeps it running and up to date. Your part is to switch it on (enable its API) and decide who may use it (IAM). It is called a proxy because it stands in the middle: you connect to IAP, and IAP makes the connection to your VM on your behalf. Its **TCP forwarding** feature does that for a VM's SSH port, or any other TCP port.

Here is the trip. Your laptop connects to IAP over ordinary HTTPS. IAP checks your Google sign-in and asks IAM whether you may reach that VM on that port. Only then does it open a connection to the VM's internal IP, from its own range `35.235.240.0/20`, across Google's network and never the public internet. The VM needs no external IP, and its firewall lets port 22 in from IAP's range only. An attacker has two ways to try, and both end early: IAP turns away anyone who is not an allowed identity, and going straight at the VM fails because it has no public address to connect to.

<Figure wide hues={["plum", "teal", "amber", "green"]} labels={{ plum: "Google's network, which holds IAP and kade-vpc alike", teal: "kade-vpc: the part you configure", amber: "The firewall rule for IAP", green: "Your connection" }} drawing={<FigWhat />}>

A bastion asks **where you connect from**: if you can reach its IP and have a key, you are in. IAP asks **who you are**: you must sign in as a Google identity that IAM allows. That is what "identity-aware" means.

</Figure>

## The fixed range: 35.235.240.0/20

IAP's connections never come from your laptop's IP or a random Google address. They always come from `35.235.240.0/20`, so the firewall rule for IAP SSH names that range and nothing else: `allow tcp:22 from 35.235.240.0/20`. Compare the wide version, `allow tcp:22 from 0.0.0.0/0`, which lets in the whole internet.

<Figure wide hues={["plum", "teal", "amber", "green"]} labels={{ plum: "Google's network, with IAP and kade-vpc inside it", teal: "kade-vpc", amber: "The IAP-only firewall rule", green: "Your connection, through IAP" }} drawing={<FigRange />}>
Two layers guard port 22. You get through because you sign in to IAP and IAP connects for you, from its own range. Google's edge stops anyone faking IAP's addresses, and the firewall stops everyone else. The scanner only gets this far when someone has given the VM an external IP, which is exactly the team's habit from the top of this chapter.
</Figure>

This range is one of the special paths from <Ch n="4">chapter 4</Ch>. Nobody else can send from it: Google's edge drops any packet from the internet that fakes one of these addresses, and you cannot use the range for your own subnets. So a connection from this range has always passed IAP's identity check first, and a firewall rule can safely trust it. The firewall narrows the **network path** to IAP only, and IAP narrows **access** to allowed identities only: two narrow layers instead of one wide-open one.

## The three things that must all be true

IAP SSH works only when all three are true:

1. **The IAP API is enabled** on the project (`iap.googleapis.com`).
2. **Your identity holds the IAM role** `roles/iap.tunnelResourceAccessor` (or `roles/owner`, which includes it).
3. **A firewall rule allows IAP's range**: tcp:22 from `35.235.240.0/20`.

Each one is checked at a different point on the connection's path, and that decides which error you see and how fast:

<Figure wide hues={["plum", "teal", "amber", "green"]} labels={{ plum: "Google's network; checks 1 and 2 are made by IAP", teal: "Checked in kade-vpc and on the VM", amber: "The firewall rule", green: "The path, when every check passes" }} drawing={<FigGates />}>
Checks 1 and 2 happen at IAP, so IAP refuses you straight away with `4033`. Checks 3 and 4 happen on the last hop, after IAP has already accepted you, so you wait while IAP tries to reach the VM, then see `4003`.
</Figure>

They are independent, and a failure often looks like a different problem:

  <thead>
    <tr><th>#</th><th>Missing</th><th>What you see</th><th>Why it confuses</th></tr>

    <tr><td>2</td><td>The IAM role</td><td>The same `4033: not authorized`</td><td>`roles/editor` does **not** include IAP tunnel access. People assume broad access covers it</td></tr>

    <tr><td>4</td><td>(bonus) SSH is not running on the VM</td><td>Also `4003`</td><td>Same symptom as a missing rule. Check the VM's serial output</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

## IAP for web pages: IAP on the load balancer

IAP has a second form. TCP forwarding (sections 02 to 06) guards a **port**, and you reach it with gcloud. **IAP for HTTPS** guards a **web page**, and people reach it with nothing but a browser. You switch it on for a load balancer's backend service, and from then on every request to that backend must pass IAP before the load balancer forwards it. It also works directly on a Cloud Run service, with no load balancer (<Ch n="10">chapter 10</Ch>).

It is the right tool when many people need a page every day: an internal dashboard, a staging site, an admin tool. Kadé's example is kade-worker's job-queue dashboard on port 8080, published for the whole team as `https://admin.kade.lk`. It can share the shop's existing load balancer: one more host in the URL map and one more certificate. <Ch n="11">Chapters 11</Ch> and <Ch n="12.1">12.1</Ch> explain the load balancer's parts in full; here only the IAP step is new.

<Figure wide hues={["plum", "amber", "teal", "green"]} labels={{ plum: "Google's network, where the load balancer and IAP run", amber: "Load balancer parts and the firewall rule", teal: "kade-vpc and kade-worker", green: "An allowed request" }} drawing={<FigIapWeb />}>
One request to `admin.kade.lk`, numbered in the order it meets each part.
</Figure>

1. **DNS** turns `admin.kade.lk` into the load balancer's IP, `34.120.88.10`.
2. **The forwarding rule** is that IP and port 443: where the request arrives.
3. **The target proxy** ends TLS, with a certificate for `admin.kade.lk`. From here the request can be read.
4. **The URL map** sends requests for `admin.kade.lk` to the backend service `kade-admin`, which points at an instance group holding kade-worker (<Ch n="12.2">chapter 12.2</Ch>).
5. **IAP, on that backend service,** checks that you are signed in and that IAM grants you `roles/iap.httpsResourceAccessor` on it. If you pass, it forwards the request with a signed header saying who you are. If you are not signed in yet, it sends you to Google sign-in first.
6. **The firewall** lets port 8080 in from the load balancer's ranges, `35.191.0.0/16` and `130.211.0.0/22`. Not IAP's `35.235.240.0/20`: that range is only for TCP forwarding.
7. **The dashboard** checks the signed header before trusting it.

### The two kinds of IAP

They share a name and a sign-in, and almost everything else differs:

<Figure wide hues={["plum", "amber", "teal"]} labels={{ plum: "IAP: the identity check", amber: "The firewall rule each one needs", teal: "The VM" }} drawing={<FigIapCompare />}>
Same idea, different gates. The role, the firewall rule and the source address the VM sees all change with the kind of IAP.
</Figure>

Use TCP forwarding for your own admin work: SSH, a database query, a one-off look at a port. Use IAP for HTTPS for pages that a team opens in a browser every day.

<Key title="What IAP for HTTPS needs">

- **An HTTPS load balancer** with a certificate for the name, and a backend service for the app.
- **IAP switched on** for that backend service.
- **The IAM role** `roles/iap.httpsResourceAccessor` on the backend service, granted to a group rather than to each person.
- **A firewall rule** for the app's port from the load balancer's ranges, `35.191.0.0/16` and `130.211.0.0/22`, and nothing wider.
- **An app that verifies the signed header**, `x-goog-iap-jwt-assertion`, so a request that skips the load balancer cannot pretend to be someone.

</Key>

<Box kind="exam" title="Exam angle">

"Employees must sign in with their Google account before reaching an internal web app, without a VPN" means IAP for HTTPS on the load balancer's backend service, with `roles/iap.httpsResourceAccessor`. Watch for answers that use the TCP role `roles/iap.tunnelResourceAccessor`, or a firewall rule from IAP's `35.235.240.0/20`: both belong to TCP forwarding.

</Box>

## Back to Kadé

<Box kind="fixed" title="Fixed: finding 2, and the temporary external IPs">

Order matters, because you are changing how you reach the VMs while using that access. Today IAP works only because `0.0.0.0/0` happens to include IAP's range, so add the IAP rule and test it **before** deleting the wide rule. Delete first, and nobody can SSH to any VM until someone adds the rule from the console.

- **Step 0, the other two requirements.** The IAP API is enabled, and you hold `roles/iap.tunnelResourceAccessor` on the project. Anyone else who needs SSH gets their own grant, as narrow as the freelancer's (section 05, tab B).
- **Step 1.** Add `kade-ssh-from-iap`: tcp:22 from `35.235.240.0/20`, all instances, priority 900, logging on.
- **Step 2.** `gcloud compute ssh … --tunnel-through-iap --command="echo ok"` succeeds for kade-api-1, kade-api-2 and kade-db.
- **Step 3.** Disable `kade-allow-ssh`, confirm step 2 still works, then delete it.
- **Step 4.** Turn on the folder-level hierarchical policy from <Ch n="5.2">chapter 5.2</Ch>: deny 22 and 3389 from `0.0.0.0/0`, with `goto_next` for `35.235.240.0/20` (pass IAP's traffic on to the VPC's own rules). Now no project owner can reopen SSH to the internet.
- **No more temporary external IPs for SSH.** The admin half of finding 5 is gone. The outgoing half (apt, PayGate) is <Ch n="7">chapter 7</Ch>.

Who logs in, and as which Linux user, is still decided by SSH keys in metadata. <Ch n="6.2">Chapter 6.2</Ch> changes that.

</Box>

<Note>

**Break-glass** (an emergency way in). If you do get locked out, the serial console is the way back in. It is a text console that Google connects to the VM directly, like plugging a keyboard into the machine. It does not use the VM's network at all, so no firewall rule applies. It is off by default and needs a login password set on the VM, so decide in advance whether you want it, and turn it off again afterwards.

</Note>
