## Why the network needs protecting

Links between hosts are shared. The Kadé office's 100 Mbit/s line to LankaNet carries the staff's browsing, a video support call, a nightly backup and every order sent to kade-api. When more arrives at a router than its outgoing link can carry, packets wait in the router's <Term>queue</Term>. When the queue is full, new packets are dropped.

<Figure hues={["plum", "teal", "green", "amber"]} drawing={<FigWhy />} />

If every sender kept blasting, every drop would cause a resend, which adds more traffic, which causes more drops. In October 1986 this actually happened on the early internet: a link between two university sites fell from 32 kbit/s to about 40 bit/s, nearly all of it resends. TCP congestion control was added in response. It makes every sender regulate itself.

## A second window: cwnd

TCP keeps a second limit, the <Term>congestion window</Term> (cwnd), separate from the receiver's rwnd. rwnd reflects the receiver's capacity and is told to the sender. cwnd is the **sender's own estimate** of what the network can carry, and nobody tells it. The sender must respect both.

## Slow start

A new connection has no idea how much the network can carry, so it starts small and ramps up fast. In <Term>slow start</Term>, every ACK adds one segment to cwnd, so cwnd **doubles every round trip**. Despite the name, this is exponential growth: a quick probe upward until the first sign of trouble.

<Chart chart="slowstart" />

<Note>

**Starts at 10, not 1.** Textbooks start cwnd at 1 segment. Modern systems start at 10 (about 14 KB), which is why small responses feel instant. A kade-api response under ~14 KB arrives in the first round trip; a 60 KB one needs three round trips just from slow start. Keeping first responses small, and reusing connections that are already "warm", both help.

</Note>

## Congestion avoidance

Once cwnd passes a threshold called <Term>ssthresh</Term> (slow start threshold), TCP switches to <Term>congestion avoidance</Term>: it grows cwnd slowly, by roughly one segment per round trip. This **additive increase** gently probes for more bandwidth without causing loss. In the graph above, the curve bends from steep to gentle at ssthresh.

## Loss means "too much"

TCP cannot see router queues, so it treats **packet loss as a sign of congestion**. How it reacts depends on how the loss was noticed (<Ch n="17">chapter 17</Ch>):

<Table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Signal</th><th>What it suggests</th><th>Reaction</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>3 duplicate ACKs</td><td>One packet was dropped, but later ones got through. Mild congestion.</td><td>ssthresh = cwnd ÷ 2, cwnd = ssthresh. Carry on in congestion avoidance (**fast recovery**).</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Retransmission timeout</td><td>Nothing is getting through. Severe.</td><td>ssthresh = cwnd ÷ 2, cwnd back to 1 segment, slow start again.</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

<Caption>Cutting cwnd by a factor (halving) is the **multiplicative decrease**. It relieves the queue quickly, then growth resumes.</Caption>

## AIMD: the sawtooth, and why it is fair

Together, congestion avoidance and loss reaction give the classic <Term>AIMD</Term> pattern: **Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease**. Plotted over time, cwnd is a sawtooth: climb slowly, drop sharply on loss, climb again.

The surprising part: AIMD makes flows **share fairly** without talking to each other. Below, the backup job (already running with a big window) and a new download share the office's link. Each time the link overflows, both halve. Halving takes more from the bigger one, while both grow by the same +1, so their shares move together:

## ECN: a warning instead of a drop

Waiting for a drop is wasteful: the data must be resent, and the queue was already full for a while. <Term>ECN</Term> (Explicit Congestion Notification) lets a router **mark** a packet as "congestion building" instead of dropping it. It is a mechanism that spans IP and TCP, not an algorithm:

  <thead>
    <tr><th></th><th>Step</th><th>Where</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><Mono>1</Mono></td><td>In the handshake, both sides agree to use ECN.</td><td>TCP flags ECE and CWR in SYN / SYN-ACK (<Ch n="18">chapter 18</Ch>)</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>2</Mono></td><td>The sender marks its packets "ECN-capable".</td><td>2 ECN bits in the IP header</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>3</Mono></td><td>A router whose queue is building sets those bits to "congestion experienced" instead of dropping.</td><td>IP header</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>4</Mono></td><td>The receiver echoes the warning back on its ACKs.</td><td>TCP flag ECE</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>5</Mono></td><td>The sender cuts cwnd as if there had been a loss, and confirms it did.</td><td>TCP flag CWR</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

<Caption>Same slowdown, but no data lost and no resend needed. It only works if routers on the path support it; many now do.</Caption>

## The rule: min(cwnd, rwnd)

At any moment, the sender may have at most **min(cwnd, rwnd)** bytes in flight: the smaller of the network's limit and the receiver's limit. Both controls apply at once, and whichever is smaller is in charge:

<Chart chart="minwin" />

This is a useful debugging question: when a transfer is slow, is it the receiver (small rwnd, growing Recv-Q, zero windows) or the network (small cwnd, retransmissions)? `ss -ti` shows both.

## A little loss costs a lot

Because every loss halves cwnd, throughput depends heavily on the loss rate and on RTT. A well-known estimate (the Mathis formula) is about **1.22 × MSS ÷ (RTT × √loss rate)**. For your laptop to kade-api (MSS 1,460, RTT 41 ms):

<Chart chart="mathis" />

Going from 0.01% to 1% loss cuts the best possible speed by 10× in this estimate. Longer RTTs make it worse, which is why long-distance transfers suffer most from small amounts of loss.

## Bufferbloat: when queues are too big

Loss-based TCP keeps growing until a queue overflows. If a router has a very large queue, TCP fills it completely before seeing any loss, and every packet waits in line behind it. Throughput stays fine, but **delay** balloons. This is <Term>bufferbloat</Term>: the video support call lags badly the moment someone starts the backup upload.

<Chart chart="bloat" />

Fixes: smarter queues on routers (active queue management such as CoDel and fq\_codel, which drop or mark early), ECN, and delay-aware algorithms like BBR (next section). On an office router, "SQM" settings turn these on.

## Congestion control algorithms

The rules above are the classic ones. Real systems use refined algorithms, and you can choose them per machine:

<Table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>#</th><th>Algorithm</th><th>Signal</th><th>How it grows</th><th>Where</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>1</td><td>Tahoe (1988)</td><td>loss</td><td>Any loss → back to 1 and slow start</td><td>Historic</td></tr>
    <tr><td>2</td><td>Reno / NewReno</td><td>loss</td><td>Classic AIMD with fast recovery (the simulator's "Reno")</td><td>The textbook model</td></tr>
    <tr><td>3</td><td>CUBIC</td><td>loss</td><td>After a loss, climbs quickly back toward the old peak, flattens near it, then probes past it. Growth depends on time, not RTT.</td><td>Default on Linux, Windows and macOS</td></tr>
    <tr><td>4</td><td>BBR</td><td>measured bandwidth and RTT</td><td>Estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum RTT, and sends at that rate instead of filling queues</td><td>Google's servers; available on Linux</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

<Caption>Only the **sender's** algorithm matters for a transfer. When kade-api sends a report to your laptop, kade-api's setting decides. BBR is often a good choice for servers talking to users over long, slightly lossy paths.</Caption>

sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_available_congestion_control

</TryIt>

<TryIt n={4} title="A throughput test">

iperf3 -c SERVER -t 10

</TryIt>

</TryIt>
