## Hub: repeat everything (layer 1)

A <Term>hub</Term> is the simplest device. It has no intelligence: any signal arriving on one port is **repeated out of every other port**. It does not read MAC or IP addresses; it only copies electrical signals, so it works at layer 1 (<Ch n="11">chapter 11</Ch>).

<Figure hues={["plum", "green", "amber"]} drawing={<FigHub />}>

B and D receive the frame, see a destination MAC that is not theirs (<Ch n="7">chapter 7</Ch>), and drop it. The bandwidth was used anyway.

</Figure>

- **One shared medium.** Only one device can send at a time. If two send at once, the signals collide and both must retry after a random wait (the old Ethernet rule called CSMA/CD).
- **Half duplex:** send or receive, never both at once.
- **Everyone sees everything:** any device can capture all traffic on the hub.

All devices on a hub are one <Term>collision domain</Term>. With more devices, collisions grow quickly and throughput falls apart. Hubs are obsolete; you will meet the idea mostly in exams and old buildings.

## Switch: learn, then forward (layer 2)

A <Term>switch</Term> is the smart replacement. It reads each frame's MAC addresses (layer 2) and keeps a <Term>MAC address table</Term>: which MAC lives behind which port. It builds that table by itself:

<Table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>#</th><th>Step</th><th>What the switch does</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><Mono>1</Mono></td><td>**Learn**</td><td>A frame arrives on port 3 from MAC 3c:7c:3f:10:22:01, so that MAC is behind port 3. Write it down.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>2</Mono></td><td>**Look up**</td><td>Find the destination MAC in the table</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>3a</Mono></td><td>**Forward**</td><td>Known: send it out of that one port only</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>3b</Mono></td><td>**Flood**</td><td>Unknown, or the broadcast MAC ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff: send it out of every port except the one it came in on</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>4</Mono></td><td>**Age out**</td><td>Forget entries not seen for a while (typically 5 minutes), so moved devices are relearned</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

<Caption>Floods happen only until the switch has heard from a device once. After that, traffic between two ports touches no other port.</Caption>

- **Every port is its own collision domain,** and links are **full duplex**: send and receive at once, no collisions at all.
- **Many conversations at once:** A ↔ B and C ↔ D run in parallel at full speed.
- **Switches do not change frames** and have no IP address on the data path (managed switches have one for their admin page).

## Router: between networks (layer 3)

A <Term>router</Term> connects **different networks**. It reads the destination **IP** address (layer 3) and uses its routing table to pick the next hop (<Ch n="12">chapter 12</Ch>). Switches move frames **within** a network; routers move packets **between** networks. On the way through, a router:

<Table>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><Mono>1</Mono></td><td>Accepts only frames addressed to its own MAC (the "gateway" MAC the sender found with ARP, <Ch n="9">chapter 9</Ch>)</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>2</Mono></td><td>Removes the frame, reads the IP header, looks up the destination network (longest prefix match)</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>3</Mono></td><td>Lowers the TTL and updates the checksum; may NAT (<Ch n="10">chapter 10</Ch>) and filter (firewall)</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>4</Mono></td><td>Builds a **new frame** on the outgoing interface, with its own MAC as source and the next device's MAC as destination</td></tr>
    <tr><td><Mono>5</Mono></td><td>**Does not forward broadcasts** (section 7)</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

## Who a frame is for: unicast, broadcast, multicast

<Table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>#</th><th>Kind</th><th>Destination MAC</th><th>A switch sends it to</th><th>Examples</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>1</td><td>**Unicast**</td><td>one device's MAC</td><td>that device's port (once learned)</td><td>Almost everything: HTTP, SSH, DB queries</td></tr>
    <tr><td>2</td><td>**Broadcast**</td><td>`ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff`</td><td>every port</td><td>ARP requests (<Ch n="9">chapter 9</Ch>), DHCP Discover (<Ch n="27">chapter 27</Ch>)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>3</td><td>**Multicast**</td><td>a group MAC, e.g. `01:00:5e:…` (IPv4) or `33:33:…` (IPv6)</td><td>every port, unless the switch tracks group members (IGMP snooping)</td><td>IPv6's NDP (<Ch n="30">chapter 30</Ch>), video to many screens</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

<Caption>The fourth kind from <Ch n="12">chapter 12</Ch>, **anycast**, happens at the routing level: to a switch it looks like normal unicast.</Caption>

## Broadcast domains

A broadcast reaches every device in its network, but **routers do not forward broadcasts**. Everything a broadcast can reach is one <Term>broadcast domain</Term>. In the office, the router makes two:

<Figure hues={["plum", "teal"]} drawing={<FigBdomain />} />

This is a big reason to split large networks into subnets (<Ch n="3">chapter 3</Ch>). In one flat network of 2,000 devices, every ARP request and DHCP Discover interrupts all 2,000; as devices multiply, background broadcast traffic grows until the network slows down. Subnets keep each broadcast domain small, and give a router a place to apply firewall rules between groups.

## VLANs: several networks on one switch

Buying a separate switch for every network gets expensive. A managed switch can be split into <Term>VLANs</Term> (virtual LANs): ports 1-8 belong to VLAN 10 (staff), ports 9-16 to VLAN 20 (warehouse). They behave like two separate switches: separate MAC tables and separate broadcast domains. Traffic between VLANs must go through a router, like any traffic between networks.

<Table>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>**Access port**</td><td>Belongs to one VLAN; the connected device knows nothing about VLANs (a PC, a printer)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>**Trunk port**</td><td>Carries many VLANs on one cable, each frame marked with a 4-byte VLAN tag (**802.1Q**). Used between switches, and to the router ("router on a stick": one cable, one sub-interface per VLAN).</td></tr>
    <tr><td>**Guest WiFi**</td><td>A classic use: visitors on their own VLAN, reaching the internet but never the office printers or PCs</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</Table>

<Caption>In GCP, a VPC plays this role: separate subnets without any switch configuration, and no broadcast at all (<Ch n="9">chapter 9</Ch>).</Caption>

## Loops and spanning tree

People often connect switches with two cables for backup. Without protection, that creates a **loop**, and a single broadcast circles forever, since Ethernet frames have no TTL (<Ch n="12">chapter 12</Ch>). Each switch floods it again, copies multiply, and within seconds the whole network is saturated: a <Term>broadcast storm</Term>.

Managed switches run **Spanning Tree Protocol** (STP, or its faster version RSTP): they talk to each other, find loops, and **block** the extra links, keeping them as backups that switch on if the main path fails. Cheap unmanaged switches usually lack it, which is how one wrong cable in a small office can take everything down.
