## An IP can repeat, a MAC cannot

Say a Kadé staff member works from their own home. Their laptop also got 192.168.1.23 from their home router. That is fine: the two home networks never mix, and on the internet each home shows its own public IP (<Ch n="10">chapter 10</Ch> explains how).

<Figure hues={["plum", "teal", "green"]} drawing={<FigRepeat />} />

A MAC is different. It must be unique within any network it joins, and maker-set MACs are unique worldwide. Even the two network cards in one laptop (WiFi and Ethernet, <Ch n="7">chapter 7</Ch>) have different MACs.

## IP gets you across networks

Follow the laptop's request to kade-api hop by hop. At every router, the destination IP is used to make a routing decision: each router checks its table (<Ch n="5">chapter 5</Ch>) and picks the next step. The MAC pair is thrown away and rebuilt at every hop. Step through it:

<HopExplorer wide />

<Key title="The point">

An IP has meaning you can compute with: its network part tells every router which direction to send it. That is why it works across the whole internet.

</Key>

## MAC finishes the job locally

A MAC has no such meaning. `42:01:0a:0a:01:0a` says who made the card (or that the platform set it), not where it is. A router could not use it to pick a direction.

But once the packet reaches the right local network, the MAC is exactly what is needed: on that network, the frame is handed to the one interface whose MAC matches. In hop 4 above, kade-api accepts the frame because the destination MAC is its own.

<Key title="Scope">

A MAC only matters inside one local network, for one step of the trip.

</Key>

## Why we need both

<Cols n={2}>

<Col title="IP · across the internet">

Globally meaningful. Gets the data to the right local network, hop by hop.

Cannot pick one device on a shared wire by itself: something must turn it into a hardware address.

</Col>

<Col title="MAC · inside one network">

Picks the exact interface for the current step.

No structure to route by, so it is useless beyond the local network.

</Col>

</Cols>

One question is left. When a device needs to deliver locally, it knows the IP (of the target or the gateway) but needs the MAC. How does it get it? With <Term>ARP</Term>, which takes an IP and returns the matching MAC. That is the next chapter.
